Teacher Guide - Common Worksheet

Engineering Design Process - Research, Brainstorming, and Redesign

Review + Extension / Transfer

1. Review - Research: cause and effect

A marble reaches the finish in 3.2 s but repeatedly leaves the track at a sharp turn. Name one factor that could affect travel time and one factor that could affect reliability. For each factor, state the effect you would predict before rebuilding.
Answer: Example: Travel time - slope angle; a steeper slope can increase speed and reduce travel time. Reliability - turn radius or side-wall height; a gentler turn or taller outside wall can reduce derailment. Other well-reasoned factors are valid.
Teacher move: Press students to add the predicted effect after each factor; nouns alone are not enough.
Student discourse move: Partners compare factor + predicted effect and ask, 'What makes you think that will happen?'

2. Review - Requirement vs. research

Which note is a research/design factor rather than a requirement?
A. The base must fit inside 12 in x 12 in.
B. A very shallow slope may slow the marble, but if it is too shallow the marble may stop.
C. The structure must be free-standing.
Answer: B. It predicts how a design variable (slope) may affect performance. A and C are requirements/constraints.
Teacher move: Ask students what makes a statement testable or predictive rather than simply a rule to follow.
Student discourse move: Students defend the classification by naming the variable and the predicted outcome.

3. Review - Brainstorm for difference

Idea A is a tall zig-zag switchback track. Describe an Idea B that is meaningfully different but still tries to maximize marble travel time. Then name one tradeoff between the two ideas.
Answer: Example Idea B: a wide spiral/funnel path. Tradeoff: a spiral can create a long continuous path in less height, but it may be harder to build accurately and may increase derailment or sticking risk. Other genuinely different ideas/tradeoffs are valid.
Teacher move: Reject cosmetic changes as a second idea; look for a different path strategy, geometry, or mechanism.
Student discourse move: Students explain what is structurally different between the two ideas before discussing which they prefer.

4. Review - Compare brainstorms

Two teams propose:
Design A: a long, shallow switchback with side walls.
Design B: a shorter, steeper spiral with no side walls.
If the goal is the longest reliable travel time, choose which one you would prototype first and justify your choice with two engineering factors.
Answer: A defensible answer may choose either design if it considers both time and reliability. Example: Design A because the longer/shallow path may increase travel time and side walls may improve reliability. A student could choose B only with a plausible argument and stated risks.
Teacher move: Do not grade the letter choice; grade the quality of the two-factor justification.
Student discourse move: Partners challenge one factor in the other's justification and ask for a predicted failure mode.

5. Review - Evidence to redesign

Version 1 data: Trial 1 = 8 s, failed at the same sharp turn; Trial 2 = 7 s, failed at that turn; Trial 3 = 6 s, successful. Propose one specific Make It Better change and explain why the test evidence supports it.
Answer: Example: increase the turn radius or add/tall-en the outside wall at that turn. The repeated failures occurred at the same location, so changing containment or turn geometry directly targets the observed failure while preserving the rest of the path.
Teacher move: Require students to name the repeated observation before accepting the redesign.
Student discourse move: Students use the frame, 'Because the test showed ___, I would change ___, which should ___.'

6. Review - Strong redesign explanation

Which Make It Better statement gives stronger engineering evidence, and why?
1. 'I will make the track better.'
2. 'Because the marble left the outside of the second turn in two trials, I will add a taller outside wall there so the marble stays on the path.'
Answer: Statement 2. It includes a specific observation, a specific design change, and the predicted effect. Statement 1 is too general to show evidence-based reasoning.
Teacher move: Highlight the three-part chain: observation -> change -> predicted effect.
Student discourse move: Students underline the evidence, box the change, and circle the predicted effect in statement 2.

7. Extension / Transfer - Transfer: structural redesign

A long-span structure holds the load, but the center bends noticeably while the joints remain secure. Which redesign is most directly supported by the evidence?
A. Add diagonal bracing near the center.
B. Add more tape to every joint.
C. Shorten the required span.
Choose and justify.
Answer: A. The observed problem is bending at the center, while the joints are already secure. Diagonal bracing targets the weak/deforming region without changing the goal. B does not match the evidence; C changes the challenge rather than improving the design.
Teacher move: Ask, 'What part of the evidence rules out each other option?'
Student discourse move: Students eliminate one distractor by citing the evidence before defending the best redesign.

8. Extension / Transfer - Evaluate improvement quantitatively

Version 1 best successful marble time is 12 s. After adding side walls, Version 2 has three successful trials and a best time of 15 s. By how many seconds did the best time improve? What is the percent increase from Version 1? Does the evidence support the redesign? Explain.
Answer: Increase = 15 - 12 = 3 s. Percent increase = 3/12 = 0.25 = 25%. Yes: the best time increased by 25% and Version 2 also had three successful trials, so the evidence supports the redesign for both performance and reliability.
Teacher move: Press students to distinguish absolute improvement (3 s) from relative improvement (25%).
Student discourse move: Students compare calculations, then connect the number back to the engineering claim in one sentence.
