Teen Safety - Teacher Guide / Evidence Guide

7th Grade Health · Intended use: extension after mastery · Work mode: teacher choice · Research policy: teacher choice

Submitted I Can Statements

  • I can identify the 4 types of abuse
  • I can identify a safe adult
  • I can identify characteristics of healthy vs. unhealthy relationships
  • I can explain how violence affects individuals, families, and communities
  • I can identify ways to keep myself safe and avoid dangerous or violent situations
  • I can analyze the effects of social media, the risks it poses to teens, and describe ways I can improve the culture at my school
Content note: The request does not define which “four types of abuse” taxonomy was taught. The student card therefore tells students to use the four-type framework already taught in class rather than substituting a potentially different framework.

DOK 1 - Recall and Reproduction

Exact student task

Build a Safety Foundations list.

  1. Name and briefly describe the four types of abuse taught in class.
  2. List three qualities that make an adult a safe adult.
  3. List two signs of a healthy relationship and two warning signs of an unhealthy relationship.
  4. Name two choices that can reduce risk in a dangerous or violent situation.

I Can mapping: 1, 2, 3, 5.

Why DOK 1: Students retrieve and reproduce foundational knowledge without needing to select among competing strategies.

Convincing evidence: Correct class-specific abuse categories; safe-adult qualities such as trustworthy, listens, respects safety/privacy limits, and knows how to get help; accurate healthy/unhealthy relationship signs; and realistic risk-reduction choices.

Weak evidence / misconceptions: Naming a “safe adult” only because the person is older; treating jealousy, control, humiliation, threats, or pressure as signs of caring; or describing a risky choice as “handling it alone.”

DOK 2 - Skills and Concepts

Exact student task

Classify and respond to each situation. For A-C, label it healthy, unhealthy, or unsafe; cite one clue; then give one reasonable next step.

A. A friend keeps posting an embarrassing screenshot after being asked to stop.

B. Two friends disagree, listen to each other, respect a boundary, and work out a compromise.

C. A student feels threatened on the way home and is deciding whether to tell a coach, counselor, or family member.

For one situation, explain why your chosen adult or action is a safer choice than doing nothing.

I Can mapping: 2, 3, 5, 6.

Why DOK 2: Students classify situations using learned concepts, identify relevant clues, and apply safety principles to familiar contexts.

Convincing evidence: A = unhealthy/unsafe because a stated boundary is ignored and online harm continues; B = healthy because boundaries, listening, and compromise are present; C = unsafe or potentially unsafe, with help-seeking from a safe adult as an appropriate response. Reasoning should connect the clue to the classification.

Product-neutral expectation: Students must show the classification, clue, next step, and one comparison to doing nothing regardless of product format.

DOK 3 - Strategic Thinking

Exact student task

Analyze this scenario: A group chat starts with jokes about a student. The jokes become repeated insults and threats. Some students repost the messages; others stay silent because they are afraid of becoming targets.

  1. Explain likely effects on the individual, family, and school community.
  2. Compare two possible responses.
  3. Choose and defend the safer response using at least three pieces of reasoning from class learning.
  4. Identify a safe adult who could help and explain why.

I Can mapping: 2, 3, 4, 5, 6.

Why DOK 3: Students must weigh plausible responses, connect consequences across multiple levels, and justify a strategy rather than follow a fully specified procedure.

Convincing evidence: Effects may include fear, isolation, distraction, stress, family concern, damaged trust, normalization of cruelty, reduced sense of safety, or increased conflict. Safer responses can include preserving evidence when appropriate, disengaging from the harmful thread, using platform reporting tools, seeking a safe adult, and following school procedures. The defense should explain why the selected response reduces risk and avoids escalating harm.

Weak evidence: “Ignore it” with no analysis; retaliating publicly; blaming the target; or assuming silence means the behavior is harmless.

DOK 4 - Extended Thinking

Exact student task

Design, test, and revise a Teen Safety Culture Plan for your school.

  1. Choose one school-culture problem connected to relationships, violence prevention, safe-adult access, or social media.
  2. Create a plan with three concrete actions, the audience for each, and the evidence/rationale for why each action could help.
  3. Include one barrier or unintended effect and how you would reduce it.
  4. Get feedback from a peer or teacher, revise at least one part, and explain what changed and why.
  5. End with a short defense of how your plan could make students safer without blaming targets of harm.

I Can mapping: 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, with the class abuse framework available as relevant background from target 1.

Why DOK 4: Students synthesize multiple safety concepts into a designed solution, anticipate consequences, seek feedback, revise, and defend a final plan. The iteration is essential; this is not simply a longer DOK 3 response.

Convincing evidence: The plan identifies a specific, school-relevant problem; proposes feasible actions; links each action to a clear safety rationale; includes access to trusted adults or reporting pathways when appropriate; anticipates a barrier; documents meaningful feedback; and explains a substantive revision.

Examples of strong actions: a safe-adult visibility system, a student-created digital conduct campaign, a clear bystander-to-upstander response routine, a reporting/help-seeking map, or a peer norm campaign that distinguishes healthy disagreement from humiliation, threats, pressure, or control.

Evidence Standard Across Product Choices

Allowed products are: Written response; Slides / presentation; Video; Audio / podcast; Graphic organizer / diagram; Poster / infographic / visual; Model / demonstration; Discussion / debate.

The format may change, but the evidence does not. DOK 4 still requires a defined problem, three actions, rationale/evidence for each, a barrier or unintended effect, feedback, revision, and a final defense. A poster with attractive slogans but missing this reasoning is not complete evidence.

Pacing and Facilitation

  • DOK 1-2: Can be completed in a short class segment as a readiness check before deeper analysis.
  • DOK 3: Best with time for students to compare options before committing to a response.
  • DOK 4: Requires authentic extended thinking. Because no time limit was supplied, allow enough time for planning, feedback, revision, and final defense; this may span more than one class period.
  • Work mode: Teacher choice. Individual work protects independent evidence; pairs or small groups can work if each student's reasoning remains visible.
  • Research: Teacher choice. Outside research is not required by the card. If used, direct students to teacher-approved, age-appropriate sources.
Safety-sensitive facilitation: Keep tasks hypothetical and do not require personal disclosure. If a student raises a real safety concern, follow school/district reporting and support procedures rather than treating the disclosure as an academic example.

Resources, Visuals, and Limitations

  • No supporting source files were attached, and no graph or diagram is needed for the task.
  • The request did not specify a target reading/access level, so the card uses concise Grade 7 language without lowering the academic demand.
  • The exact four-type abuse taxonomy remains intentionally class-specific because the request did not define it.
  • No unresolved instructional conflict remains: all six I Can statements are represented across the integrated DOK progression.
